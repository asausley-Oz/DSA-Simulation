# Shared Collective Cosmology (SCC)

A cross-cultural framework for understanding the universal cosmological structures that appear across human religious and philosophical traditions — and how the biblical narrative both reflects and transcends them.

## Overview

Shared Collective Cosmology (SCC) is a theoretical framework that identifies a common cosmological structure underlying the religious, philosophical, and mythological traditions of cultures across time and geography. It proposes that humanity, broadly speaking, shares an intuitive understanding of reality organized around three interconnected realms — the heavenly, the natural, and the underworld — and that this shared structure points to something deeper than cultural coincidence.

SCC is not syncretism. It does not collapse all religions into one. Rather, it uses the universality of this cosmological structure as a lens for understanding why the biblical narrative resonates so deeply across cultures — and why it alone offers a resolution to the cycles that every other tradition can only describe.

## The Three-Realm Structure

At the core of SCC is a tripartite cosmological architecture present in virtually every major cultural and religious tradition:

| Realm | Character | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Upper / Heavenly | Source of life, purity, divine order | Heaven (Hebrew), Brahman (Hindu), the Forms (Platonic), Tian (Chinese) |
| Middle / Natural | Convergence of heaven and underworld; life and corruption coexist | The material world across nearly all traditions |
| Lower / Underworld | Chaos, death, corruption, the adversarial | Sheol (Hebrew), Hades (Greek), Naraka (Hindu/Buddhist), the Abyss |

The natural realm is the convergence zone — where the life force descending from the highest heaven meets the chaos and corruption ascending from below. This is why the natural world contains both extraordinary beauty and profound darkness simultaneously. Both love and violence, creation and decay, goodness and evil inhabit the same space.

## The Corrupting Nature of the Material Realm

A consistent observation across SCC traditions is that the material realm is understood as inherently corrupting — not in the sense that matter itself is evil, but that existence within it involves exposure to forces of decay, distortion, and distance from the pure source.

This appears in:

* **Platonism** — the material world as a shadow of the true Forms; the body as a prison of the soul
* **Gnosticism** — the material realm as the creation of a lesser or malevolent demiurge
* **Hinduism** — maya (illusion) as the veil that obscures true reality; the material world as the realm of attachment and suffering
* **Buddhism** — samsara as the cycle of suffering driven by desire and ignorance
* **Hebrew cosmology** — the world east of Eden as the realm of the curse; distance from God as the defining feature of post-Fall existence

SCC does not endorse the dualistic conclusions of Gnosticism or the escapism of certain Eastern traditions. But it takes seriously that these observations are nearly universal — and that they are pointing at something real.

## The Circular / Cyclical Nature of the Systems

One of the most significant observations within SCC is that the systems within it are circular and cyclical. They do not resolve. They repeat. They spiral without terminus.

This cyclical quality appears in:

* The Hindu yugas — vast cosmic cycles of creation, sustenance, and destruction
* The Buddhist wheel of samsara — birth, death, and rebirth without end
* The Greek eternal return and tragic cycles of hubris and nemesis
* The Mesopotamian flood narratives repeating themes of creation and destruction
* The Norse Ragnarok — the end that resets rather than resolves
* The secular cycles of civilization — rise, peak, decline, collapse, rise again

Even within the biblical narrative itself, SCC identifies these cycles: Eden -> Fall -> partial restoration -> further fall -> covenant -> rebellion -> exile -> return -> rebellion. The cycles are real. They are not resolved by human effort, religious practice, or philosophical insight alone.

The key question SCC raises is: **What, if anything, breaks the cycle?**

## The Life Force and Its Descent

SCC traces a consistent pattern in which life originates from the highest heaven — from the creator or the source of the heavens — and descends into the natural order. As it descends, it becomes diluted, mixed, or corrupted by the convergence with lower forces.

This pattern is present in:

* The Hebrew ruach and nephesh — the breath of life breathed into Adam from above
* The Platonic psyche descending from the realm of Forms into matter
* The Hindu atman — the individual soul as a fragment of Brahman, the universal life force
* The Gnostic pneuma — the divine spark trapped in material existence
* Indigenous and shamanic traditions of a sky-father or creator spirit as the source of life

SCC does not equate these concepts — they differ significantly in their conclusions. But it notes that the intuition of a life force descending from a pure heavenly source is nearly universal.

## Atheism as a Surface Phenomenon

Within the SCC framework, atheism is a relatively recent and historically thin layer of human cosmological thought — existing meaningfully only in the last few hundred years, and even then primarily in post-Enlightenment Western contexts.

SCC uses the image of an apple:

* **The skin** represents atheism — the outermost, most recent layer; thin, covering, but not constitutive
* **The flesh** represents the shared cosmological intuitions — the deep, cross-cultural sense of realms, life force, curse, and spiritual reality that most of humanity has held across history
* **The core** represents the post-Ice Age world where these cosmological ideas appear to have crystallized and taken their earliest organized forms

This framing is not a dismissal of atheism as a philosophical position. It is an observation about its cosmological novelty — and a suggestion that the deeper human default is not atheism but a robust, if often distorted, spiritual cosmology.

## SCC and the Biblical Narrative

The biblical narrative does not invent the three-realm cosmological structure — it inhabits and redeems it. Scripture speaks the language of heaven, earth, and the underworld fluently. It acknowledges the corrupting power of the natural realm, the cyclical nature of human failure, and the descent of life from above.

What the biblical narrative does that no other tradition does is **break the cycle from within**:

* Not by escaping the material realm (Gnosticism, certain forms of Buddhism)
* Not by endlessly managing karma or merit (Hindu/Buddhist frameworks)
* Not by resigning to eternal recurrence (Greek/Norse models)
* But by God entering the cycle as a human being, bearing its full weight, dying within it, and rising out of it — inaugurating a new creation that is not subject to the same decay

This is the resolution that SCC itself cannot generate — it can only describe the problem. The gospel is the answer the framework itself is asking for.

## SCC and Covenant Distance Theology (CDT)

SCC provides the universal background against which CDT makes its fullest sense. The cycles that SCC identifies in all human cosmological traditions are the same cycles that CDT traces within the biblical narrative itself. The difference is that the biblical narrative — read through CDT — does not merely participate in the cycles. It exposes them, enters them, and breaks them.

## SCC and Shared Intuitions: Key Parallels

| Theme | Biblical | Hindu | Greek / Platonic | Buddhist | Mesopotamian |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heavenly source of life | Ruach, Creator | Brahman, Prana | The Forms, the One | Nirvana, pure awareness | An / sky-father |
| Material corruption | Curse, exile from Eden | Maya, samsara | The cave, the body as tomb | Suffering, impermanence | Chaos beneath creation |
| Cyclical failure | Judges cycle, exile/return | Yugas | Eternal return, tragedy | Wheel of rebirth | Flood/creation cycles |
| Remnant / awakened ones | The remnant of Israel | Jnani, the liberated | The philosopher | The bodhisattva | The flood survivor |
| Resolution | New creation in Christ | Moksha (liberation) | Return to the Forms | Parinirvana | Establishment of order |

SCC does not equate these resolutions. It notes their family resemblance while insisting that the biblical resolution is categorically different — not an escape from the cycle, but a death and resurrection within it.

## Implications for Theology and Mission

SCC has significant implications for how the gospel is communicated cross-culturally, particularly in Eastern contexts:

* The cosmological intuitions people already hold are not the enemy — they are partial maps pointing toward a territory the gospel fully reveals
* Concepts like karma, curse, and spiritual distance are not obstacles to the gospel — they are entry points for it
* The cyclical frustration that Eastern traditions name so well — the sense that the wheel keeps turning and nothing finally resolves — is precisely what the gospel addresses
